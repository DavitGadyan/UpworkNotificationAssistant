--- Negotiation Is Not ---
Before we talk about structure for successful negotiations, it's important to note that negotiation is not a battle. Negotiation is not an argument, it's not an opportunity for win-lose, and it's important to state why. You see, you might well read articles or books that give you strategies how to create deals that give you an advantage, but in the long run, question is, do you want to do deals in the future with these people? So if you create a deal where you win and someone else loses, the reality is they're not minded to want to do a deal with you in the future. The other thing, and this is important if you're in an agency or you're in any kind of relationship where you have clients, you also want to really watch doing deals where you lose, because if you do a deal where you lose and the other person feels like they win, then they want to replicate that in the future. They kind of feel that every time they negotiate with you, they'll need to get that little bit more and insist you give that little bit more and they give less. So it's important to recognise that negotiation is about creating what we call win-win, creating a deal that works for every party. It's also important to recognise it shouldn't be a substitute for selling. It shouldn't be an excuse for working out whether the person really wants to do business with you. If somebody's not ready to do business, then it's not worth negotiating and we'll come on to this when we think about entering the discussion phase, because in the discussion phase we make sure that the person we're negotiating with is clear they want to do a deal first. The other thing says it's not haggling and in the next lecture I'll distinguish between haggling and negotiating because really it's a very different thing.

--- Step Two – Preparation: Understanding The Power Of Variables ---
For our next step, we start thinking about creating a plan for our negotiation. But before we start preparing our plan, we need to understand about variables. So this lecture is going to talk about what are variables, what makes a good variable, and why variables are really such an important part of a good negotiation. So a variable, put simply, is something that can change in a package in order to get a better deal for either party. You can distinguish a variable from a constant. That's something that can't change. So if I'm buying a motorbike, for example, well, I've got to have the motorbike. The variable might be which tyres they put on it. The variable might be whether I get the ABS brakes. The thing about a variable, it's only a variable if you can trade it. So if it's something you are prepared to take out. So for example, I was working with a business recently, and they said, well, one variable is our wonderful customer service. And I said, well, would you be prepared to give people bad customer service? And they said, well, no, we always give everybody great customer service. So that's not a variable. You can't say to somebody, well, we'll give you good customer service. Well, no, if you don't pay this, we won't. That doesn't seem to work. Does that make sense? So a variable is something that you can trade, and you're prepared to trade. Now, in the best-selling book ever on negotiation, Getting to Yes, Roger Fisher talks about searching for creative options. What he means here is searching for variables. So he distinguishes this idea. He suggests that all of the research that he's done at the Harvard Business School points towards that the more you search for creative options, the more you really dig around and find good variables to use, the better deals you create. An example of this might simply be a parking space. You might have three parking spaces in your apartment lot, and you might be renting an apartment out. Well, if you can offer one of these free parking spaces, that might be really high value to somebody. But the interesting thing is somebody might come who commutes on the tube all the day. Well, that wouldn't be a good variable to use. So the important thing about variables is they've got high value to one party while not costing too much to the other party. --- Power Dynamics In Negotiation  ---
Welcome back. In the previous lecture, we studied how your emotional intelligence can impact negotiations. Here, in Lecture 4, we'll discuss power dynamics in negotiation. So let's get started. Power dynamics during negotiations refers to who has the greater bargaining power and as a result is more likely to achieve their desired results. This imbalance in power can make you feel disadvantaged, particularly when the power is not in your favour. This can lead you to make unethical decisions in such instances, but we'll discuss ethics further in Lecture 6. Nonetheless, to be an effective negotiator, you must be able to shift the power to you, but to do so, you must first know the other party's bargaining power. You need to know the source of their bargaining power to shift the dynamics to your side. To this end, let's examine some key power sources during negotiations. Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement The best alternative to a negotiated agreement – or BATNA – is one of the greatest power sources during negotiations. Simply put, this power source allows you to walk away from discussions at any given time if it's not in your favour, as you have options. For example, you must hire a decorator for your company's upcoming business event. After sending the bids, you receive some competitive offers from qualified decorators. In this case, you have the bargaining power as you can walk away from discussions with any of the vendors as you have alternatives that you can turn to. The vendors are also likely to offer you more, so that you hire them. Knowing the Other Party's BATNA Knowing the other party's BATNA is another good source of bargaining power. This helps shape the discussions. You must know what their alternatives are and what actions they're likely to take if discussions don't go their way. They're left in a weaker position if they have no other options. As a result, doing your research before negotiations begin is imperative. Expert Power The more knowledge you have and can use during negotiations, the more leverage you have. You must be able to use the information you have, otherwise it's useless. This is the source of expert power – being able to use this information. In this case, your organisational position doesn't matter. For example, the store manager may have expert power over the CEO regarding the store's daily operations. Position Power Position power is obtained based on your organisational position. Based on your position in a company, you may have more power in a given situation than a counterpart in a similar position. It could also mean that a higher position gives you more power. For example, the purchasing manager is more likely to influence decisions made in the purchasing department than the sales manager. Position power can also occur, for example, when the CEO's decision overrides the general manager's. Nonetheless, position power is only effective if you're respected and held in high regard. Otherwise, your position may be meaningless. Organisational Power This type of negotiation power happens when a negotiator relies on their company's reputation or the weight of the brand to gain more leverage during negotiations. It could also be used to reference power from your organisational position in your company. Let's see how Starbucks tried to use their organisational power to gain leverage during negotiations with Kraft. In 1998, Kraft Foods and Starbucks entered a deal to distribute Starbucks packaged coffee in supermarkets. As the coffee industry evolved and more coffee products like single-serve pods became available, Starbucks wanted to get out of the deal to take advantage of the situation. To this end, it attempted to buy out the contract for $750 million in 2010. However, Kraft was against this, and despite Kraft's objection, Starbucks proceeded to end the contract. The two companies tried unsuccessfully to mediate the situation. In the end, in 2013, Kraft ultimately won when an arbitrator ruled that Starbucks breached the contract and had to pay Kraft $2.75 billion to settle the issue. Starbucks tried to use its weight and significant growth in the coffee industry to strong-arm Kraft out of the deal, but ultimately it was a rather expensive decision. To this end, organisational power should be used sparingly during negotiations and only after considerable thought and when you're sure that the outcome is favourable. We've just discussed various sources of power in negotiations. When you have more bargaining power, it's easier for you to walk away, but sometimes working with the other party can be beneficial, particularly if your goal aligns with theirs. Consider both your long- and short-term goals. On the other hand, you may not always have the required leverage or share the same desired results. In such instances, the most effective strategy is one that showcases your value and what you can bring to the table. In the end, you can determine the best negotiation strategy when you consider your goals, power dynamics, the type of relationship you share, and the value of the relationship. What's culturally acceptable also depends on the other party's age and upbringing. For example, an older Japanese man may be less accepting of certain gestures than a younger male. You don't want to assume what the other party will do and vice versa. Instead, be open-minded and willing to adjust your strategy as needed.
